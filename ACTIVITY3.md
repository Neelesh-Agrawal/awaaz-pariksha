Who benefits, and roughly how many

Awaaz Pariksha is for blind and low-vision students in India, from Class 6 through college, who use a scribe or other assistance to complete written work. Census 2011 recorded about 50 lakh people with a disability in seeing, but that includes all ages and does not tell us how many students use scribes. UDISE+ 2025-26 counts about 22 lakh children with special needs in school. We would start with a one-term pilot for 30 students across three schools, aiming to include English, Hindi and Tamil speakers. Families could spend less time arranging help for practice, and teachers would receive readable answer sheets.

What changes compared with today

A student who relies on a scribe may need to arrange help, ask for an answer to be read back, and request a correction. They usually cannot check independently what was written, and practice waits until a helper is free.

With Awaaz Pariksha, the student can:
- hear the question;
- dictate in their own words;
- listen to what was captured;
- say "delete under" or "change biogas to biofuel" to fix a mistake without repeating the whole answer;
- submit by voice.

The app captures speech; it does not generate, improve or suggest answers. The goal is more control over when and how students practise, and the ability to verify their own answers, while keeping their usual accommodations available.

How we'd know it worked

Students would try comparable practice papers using the prototype and their usual method. Targets:
- By the third session, at least 24 of 30 students complete and submit a mixed paper without another person's help.
- Students can fix one word in a 200-word answer in under 30 seconds.
- Saved answers survive a reload or simulated crash.
- We aim for 90% word accuracy before edits and 98% after student corrections. With consent, we'll compare the raw transcript with a human transcript of the recording, and the final sheet with the wording the student confirms they intended to submit.

We'd separately check whether students can spot and fix errors that change the meaning, and ask which method they would choose for practice and why.

The main risk

Speech recognition can mishear regional accents, mixed-language answers and technical terms, especially in noisy rooms. One missed word can change an answer's meaning. We would test those conditions, offer headsets, make read-back and correction easy, and flag low-confidence text for review, without treating those flags as a guarantee. Browser recognition may need internet, so we would start with homework and supervised practice. This prototype is not approved for official exams.
